# Mission Intelligence

Mission Intelligence describes how observations become trusted support for decisions and learning.

## Lifecycle

```text
Observation
    ↓
Information
    ↓
Knowledge
    ↓
Understanding
    ↓
Intelligence
    ↓
Decision
    ↓
Action
    ↓
Learning
```

The lifecycle is not always linear. New evidence may challenge earlier knowledge, and learning may require a previous decision to be revisited.

The lifecycle should not be interpreted as a claim that useful knowledge arises only through formal data processing. Lived experience, local and situated knowledge, tacit expertise, cultural knowledge and other legitimate forms of knowing may contribute at multiple stages.

## Observation

An observation is a recorded event, measurement, statement or artefact.

Examples include:

- a satellite image
- an AIS position
- a weather report
- a government publication
- a sensor reading
- a human observation
- lived or situated experience
- tacit observations from an experienced operator or practitioner

Observations should retain time, source, context and provenance whenever possible.

## Information

Observations become information when context and meaning are added.

Information should distinguish recorded facts from interpretation and assumptions.

## Knowledge

Knowledge emerges when information is connected, tested and placed within a useful model of the mission.

Knowledge remains revisable when evidence changes.

Formalisation can make knowledge easier to communicate and process, but can also hide forms of knowledge that the chosen model cannot represent. Absence from the model is not evidence of absence from reality.

Access to knowledge is not automatically an entitlement to possess, reproduce or disclose it. Consent, stewardship, privacy, security, cultural sovereignty and legitimate restrictions should be respected where applicable.

## Understanding

Understanding explains relevant relationships, causes, constraints and uncertainty.

It addresses questions such as:

- What happened?
- Why might it have happened?
- What changed?
- What remains unknown?
- What could invalidate this interpretation?
- What knowledge or experience may be missing from the model?
- Which perspectives could materially change our interpretation?

## Perspective Challenge

Before consequential understanding is shaped into intelligence, relevant perspectives should be deliberately sought and allowed to challenge the emerging interpretation.

Useful questions include:

- Who knows?
- What counts as knowing here?
- Who is heard?
- Who is missing?
- Who defined the language of the problem?
- Who benefits?
- Who may be harmed?
- Who carries the risk?
- Who will live with the consequences?
- What would have to be true for our interpretation to be wrong?

Perspective diversity is a mechanism for epistemic resilience, not a claim that all statements are equally supported. Evidence, provenance, context and uncertainty remain essential.

Perspectives may include scientific and technical expertise, lived experience, local and Indigenous knowledge, operators and maintainers, affected communities, cultural and historical knowledge, moral and philosophical reasoning, creative exploration and artificial intelligence.

The purpose is not necessarily consensus. Productive disagreement should remain visible and traceable.

## Intelligence

Intelligence is knowledge and understanding shaped for a defined decision or action.

It should communicate:

- relevant evidence
- provenance
- confidence and uncertainty
- assumptions and limitations
- plausible alternatives
- material disagreements and missing perspectives
- recommended actions and trade-offs

Mission Framework deliberately separates intelligence from decision authority.

Increasing capability or intelligence does not itself confer legitimate authority.

## Authority Boundary

Consequential missions should make the boundary between intelligence production, decision authority and action authority explicit.

```text
Intelligence production
        ↓
---------------------------
     Authority boundary
---------------------------
        ↓
Decision authority
        ↓
Action authority
```

The strength of this boundary may depend on consequence, reversibility, mandate, uncertainty and risk.

This distinction becomes especially important as artificial intelligence becomes increasingly capable of observing, reasoning, planning and acting.

## Decision

A decision selects a course of action within a defined mandate and risk context.

Mission Framework supports decisions. It does not remove human accountability for consequential choices.

Decision processes should make clear who has authority, who is affected, what significant disagreements remain, and how challenge or appeal can occur where appropriate.

## Action

Actions should remain traceable to their decision, rationale, evidence and accountable owner.

Action should also preserve visibility of consequences that fall outside the original success measure. Aggregate success must not conceal materially distributed harm.

## Learning

Every completed action may create new observations and evidence.

Learning should improve the current mission and, where appropriate, contribute reusable knowledge to the wider framework.

Consequences should be allowed to challenge the original understanding, assumptions and decision — not merely measure whether the planned action was completed.

## Human and Artificial Intelligence

Mission Framework anticipates missions in which human and artificial intelligence increasingly work together.

The intended model is not simply:

```text
Human → AI → Answer
```

It is a reciprocal process in which human intention, lived experience, values, imagination and accountability may interact with artificial intelligence capable of retrieval, pattern discovery, synthesis, simulation and generation of alternatives.

Humans should be able to challenge artificial intelligence. Artificial intelligence should be able to challenge human assumptions. Evidence should be able to challenge both. Consequences should be able to challenge the decision that followed.

These categories are working descriptions, not permanent claims about capabilities unique to humans or machines.

The research foundation for this developing concept of hybrid intelligence is maintained in `INTELLECTUAL_CORPUS.md`.

## Trust throughout the Lifecycle

Trust is not a final quality check. It must be designed into every transition:

- observation to information
- information to knowledge
- knowledge to understanding
- understanding through perspective challenge
- understanding to intelligence
- intelligence across the authority boundary
- decision to action
- action to consequence and learning

Trust does not require agreement. It requires that consequential disagreement can be represented, challenged, traced and carried responsibly into decision-making.

This is **Trust by Design** applied to mission intelligence.
